CLASSIFYING A TISSUE SAMPLE ONE DNA FRAGMENT AT A TIME

Assigning a DNA fragment to its most likely cell of origin gets harder as the number of possible cell types grows. The solution turned out to be letting the model know when to remain unsure. 

Published 15 September 2026
Authors Rizdvanetskyi, Roos, Lutsik
Read the paper More from the lab
Summary

In short

  • The aim is to achieve deconvolution of the tumor ecosystem which involves estimating the proportions of the malignant cell populations together with the surrounding microenvironment. To get there, we choose to focus on epigenetic information, beginning with DNA methylation and then including other signals. This could eventually help track tumor composition, disease progression, and treatment response. Getting there requires methods that can scale to hundreds of cell types without discarding the information contained in individual DNA reads.
  • A methylation pattern does not always identify a single cell type, especially as the number of cell types grows. In our 39-cell-type setting, roughly 97% of reads were uninformative for distinguishing individual cell types, making conventional hard-label classification a poor fit for the problem.
  • Instead of forcing each read into one class, we represent it using a distribution over cell types derived from the methylation patterns observed in the reference. We call this approach data-driven soft labeling. Syto builds on this idea to combine read-level classification with proportion estimation and calibration, providing a framework for scaling DNA methylation deconvolution to larger reference panels.
  • Across 360 configurations, the best Syto setup reduced mean squared error on simulated mixtures by 3.7-fold relative to the strongest baseline, while also improving concordance on an independent 16-tissue atlas. 
  • This work focuses on normal cell types; extending the reference to malignant populations is an important next step toward tumor ecosystem deconvolution. Longer reads and multimodal epigenetic data could provide additional information for distinguishing cell types, while validation on real mixtures remains necessary before clinical applications can be assessed.

Part IThe context

What makes cells different?

The human body is a complex organism combining more than thirty trillion cells working in synergy to support a vast network of co-dependent processes. Different organs are composed of various tissues which, in turn, harbor different cell types. Some are highly specific, like cardiomyocytes found only in the heart or sex cells like sperm and egg; others are more diverse, like epithelial cells covering everything from skin to the internal surface of our organs. When your author was studying biology in school, about two hundred “normal” cell types were known. These days, the estimate is at least double this number and growing, as technology allows us to capture diverse functions of previously undifferentiated cells.

This has largely become possible thanks to molecular analyses and methods that apply them at single-cell resolution, measuring things like DNA composition (genomics), gene transcription (transcriptomics), protein production (proteomics), and traces of cellular processes (metabolomics) — collectively referred to as -omics. Cardiomyocytes and neurons, for example, produce different proteins and therefore transcribe different genes. These signals can be used to distinguish cell types. DNA, however, is largely the same in every cell of our body and contains the full set of instructions for every possible cell type. The question is: how does each cell know which genes to express?

Epigenomics
Sits on the genome layer and targets DNA methylation, histone modifications, and chromatin accessibility. Assays include WGS with bisulfite conversion (WGBS), nanopore sequencing, ChIP-seq, ATAC-seq, and many others. In our paper, we rely on WGBS methylation data for training, but in principle, Syto can work with different epigenetic signals with necessary adaptations ranging from none to moderate.

Further down the chain, signals change faster and vary more between cells of the same type. DNA is also the most chemically stable of the four molecules, which is why fragments of it survive in blood plasma.

How does DNA tell us which cell is which?

The full answer is complex, but DNA spatial organization and accessibility are perhaps among the most important factors. Accessibility determines whether transcription machinery can reach DNA and convert genes of interest into RNA, precursors of proteins. Spatial organization, in turn, can enhance or reduce transcription depending on the DNA’s three-dimensional configuration. These properties can be measured directly, for example with methods like ATAC-seq, but another question immediately arises: what drives DNA structure?

DNA accessibility and organization are largely regulated by two factors: DNA methylation and histone modifications. DNA methylation is the chemical addition of a methyl group to a cytosine that is followed by a guanine on the same strand (a CpG site). It is not a mutation because the nucleotide itself stays the same. If DNA were a text and each nucleotide a letter, DNA methylation would be an accent that does not change the letter but may affect the meaning of the larger message. Histones are protein complexes that compact DNA into chromatin — a mixture of DNA and proteins that forms chromosomes. Various histone modifications can promote chromatin compaction or opening, therefore reducing or facilitating transcription. Just as different cell types express different genes, their DNA methylation and histone modifications will also vary, with localized alterations directly attributable to changes in protein yield. In practice, it means that epigenetic factors can be used to discriminate between cell types. Perhaps even more importantly, changes in the epigenome may herald structural shifts in cell state and function well before those shifts result in altered RNA and protein production. At the same time, certain methylation signatures may remain well preserved even after cells undergo dramatic transformation, like in cancer cells that, despite a largely aberrant methylome, can be traced back to their normal cell of origin by focusing on a few stable inherited methylation domains. This is why DNA methylation and histone modification are specifically interesting targets for any type of analysis where time is an important factor, with strong applications in aging and disease.
BOX 02

Epigenetics and non-mutational reprogramming

How DNA is packaged, what additional marks exist on top and around DNA, and how cells alter their function and state without changes in the genomic sequence
Each human cell holds about two meters of DNA. An old rumor says that if you unwrap all the DNA in your body into a single long chain, you can stretch it back and forth between Earth and Pluto seventeen times. We don’t know of any technology that would let us pull off such a trick, but the fact that each copy of the genome fits into a nucleus a few micrometers across is a marvel of nature. Such extraordinary wrapping is done by protein complexes called nucleosomes, each made of four pairs of histones. Roughly 147 base pairs coil about 1.7 times around each nucleosome core. To compact even further, strings of nucleosomes fold into chromatin, and chromatin into chromosomes. How tightly this packaging is folded decides which genes the cell can access and read. Some parts of the genome are always folded; others are dynamic and made accessible when cell function requires production of specific proteins. DNA methylation is a distinct signal linked to chromatin accessibility and can act independently to repress the reading machinery, even in regions of open chromatin. In normal cells, DNA methylation is preserved after cell division, and structural chromatin changes are highly organized and orchestrated to occur at the proper time. This allows a group of cells to maintain their type and function in a given tissue.

Non-mutational epigenetic reprogramming

Cells can also change their behavior through epigenetic changes alone, without any mutation. Stress, famine, and disease may dysregulate chromatin organization, affecting downstream gene expression and protein production and eventually altering cell morphology and behavior. In cancer, this is common enough that it was added to the hallmarks of cancer framework in 20221. Global hypomethylation can activate oncogenes, while point-based hypermethylation can turn off tumor suppressor genes. Histone modifications alter chromatin structure, allowing cancer to acquire adaptive and protective properties.  Unlike mutations, epigenetic marks can be reversed, which makes them good therapy targets. Yet, left unchecked for too long, epigenetic aberrations can trigger permanent mutations in the genome, which is why the treatment efficacy also depends on timely diagnosis. This is one of the reasons why we and many others before us focused on DNA methylation and other epigenetic factors as potential targets instead of more traditional approaches like gene-expression profiling and copy number variation calling, which may not show differences at early stages of disease progression. 

Why do we care about cell-type composition?

One clinically relevant problem that relies on cell-type-specific signals is cell-type deconvolution: determining the proportions of contributing cell types in a given biological sample, whether it is a tissue slice from a solid biopsy or blood plasma. Solving this problem matters because accurate quantification of tissue composition is decision-level information for diagnosis, prognosis, and treatment selection. Healthy and malignant tissues differ drastically in cellular composition, but precise determination of cellular subtypes can further distinguish otherwise similar-looking diseases with widely different prognostic outcomes and optimal treatment options. This applies to various conditions, but even more so to cancers, where cancer cell clones and surrounding immune and stromal cells collectively form a tumor microenvironment (TME) that can be “hot” (infiltrated by immune cells and more responsive to treatment) or “cold” (inaccessible to immune cells and favorable to cancer growth)2 susceptible to treatment or inaccessible to immune infiltration and fertile for cancer proliferation. While constituent cell proportions inform diagnosis at a given time point, observing longitudinal changes in TME composition allows us to assess treatment efficacy and overall disease progression and can potentially be used for routine monitoring during recovery, provided that underlying technology is both available and accessible. Therefore, the results of tumor ecosystem deconvolution, which estimates the proportions of tumor clones together with the cells of the surrounding tumor microenvironment (TME), represent a valuable input for clinical analysis in addition to traditional methods like pathology.

Why not just measure every cell?

Worldwide, research labs increasingly rely on single-cell multi-omics methods to produce comprehensive TME profiles. This single-cell granularity means researchers can annotate each sampled cell individually and, more often than not, map it to a specific location in the tissue. Case by case, it contributes to the creation of single-cell atlases that will undoubtedly be extremely useful references for future methods development. But in 2026, as we write this, single-cell profiling is too cumbersome, slow, and costly to use in clinics en masse. Cheaper, faster, and easier-to-execute protocols are being put into practice, largely through collaboration between labs and clinics, but fully independent clinical pipelines are becoming increasingly possible. The technology is a major driver of this trend, with Oxford Nanopore sequencing setting a new bar for the speed and cost of genome profiling and improving potential accessibility and affordability for patients. In 2023, DNA methylation signals collected using ONT platforms were used for ultra-fast classification of central nervous system (CNS) tumors, with results available as fast as 80 minutes after sample collection.
BOX 03

Single-cell versus bulk sequencing

Measuring cells one at a time or all at once, and the two generations of bulk sequencing.
Single-cell
Cells are separated first, and each gets its own barcode so the final sequencing results can be traced back to their source. The technology is popular in research laboratories focused on generating large amounts of new data, as it enables precise annotation and characterization of the study's tissues and organs, supporting discovery. Single-cell sequencing and related but distinct single-nucleus sequencing are go-to technologies for detailed profiling. They are increasingly used to create comprehensive molecular references or atlases of the human body. Limitations include low yield per cell, high cost, and long turnaround times, making them impractical for clinical use cases.    
Bulk
In bulk sequencing, one sequences the whole biological sample at once. The DNA is read in fragments, commonly called reads, and, unlike single-cell methods, it is not possible to know whether two distinct reads come from the exact same cell. Bulk libraries are typical inputs to various computational methods, including deconvolution algorithms. Bulk sequencing is significantly cheaper and faster than single-cell sequencing and is increasingly used in clinical settings.

From cancer classification to tissue composition

More recently, pan-cancer (capable of generalizing beyond a single cancer group) deep learning classifiers trained on multi-platform methylation data3,4,5 have emerged to broaden possible application contexts. Classification alone, however, does not allow examination of TME content. Classifier creators are increasingly investing in augmenting models with explainability tools to compensate for this lack of context. For example, CrossNN5 design allows a direct connection between specific CpG methylation status and prediction outcomes. Interpreting these connections requires additional work: checking important CpGs, examining related genes, and consulting the literature. While valuable for biologists, explaining classification results by analyzing the importance of individual CpGs may be less appealing to clinicians, especially in the intraoperative constrained environment. A more relatable measure, with a broader set of estimation methods, is the proportion of cancer cells in a given sample, also called tumor purity and applicable to solid biopsies; or the proportion of the circulating tumor DNA in the cell-free DNA found in the bloodstream, also called tumor fraction. Existing case studies using methylomes for purity estimates are typically limited to specific cancer families, but we believe pan-cancer versions are undoubtedly in the making. At first glance, cancer classification combined with purity estimates sounds like a better package than classification alone — purity can be used to make inferences about disease progression or efficacy of treatment. Yet, as Haider et al.6 show (and we agree), purity estimates suffer from low concordance between methods and hide important heterogeneity in TME composition. Still, an accurate tumor purity estimate is useful for paremetrization and interpretation of genomic analysis6, but on its own it answers a narrow question. Tumor ecosystem deconvolution, by contrast, is a different story. Assuming the recovered profiles are correct, tumor purity is trivially derived, and cancer type can be predicted using the obtained proportions as features, providing an interpretability layer that pathologists can directly cross-link with H&E slides.

What would we want from a deconvolution method?

Above all, it must be accurate, but it must also be convenient, which means, among many other things, broad applicability. Those criteria are real; alas, knowing them is not the same as achieving them. So, we did what people in our tribe do regularly and with gusto — we made assumptions about the qualities that are necessary for meeting those criteria, sorted them nicely and put them into a list.

Assumptions

The five criteria, explained

The assumptions behind each quality on the vision map.

1. We assumed that a good tumor ecosystem deconvolution method must scale to hundreds of cell types. For one, we don’t want a separate method for every tissue (that wouldn't be convenient anymore), and more importantly, we want granular cell typing, because coarse, high-level differentiation is not sufficient for clinical applications.

2. We also believe that the method must be pattern-specific, meaning it can independently evaluate information from every read and quantify associations between observed motifs and cell identity. Averaging read-level signal is common in the field. Still, it is not lossless compression — the notion that specific pattern changes amounting to the same average signal mean different things in cancer is a current consensus in biomedical studies.

3. Working at the read level means choosing between short- and long-read platforms. Short-read systems have been on the market for more than twenty years and were largely unrivaled for most of that time, so such data are abundant, and shelving them would be a waste. They are still widely used for single-cell sequencing, which is becoming the standard for ground-truth references. Liquid biopsies rely on fragments of circulating tumor DNA in blood, which can be sequenced with long-read platforms but still produce short fragments within the limits of next-generation sequencing. So, a good method should learn from and perform well on short reads, but the best method will also take advantage of long reads when available. Beyond the declared benefits of direct modification calling and the possibility of single-molecule multiome profiling, long-read libraries contain much more information per read. They are also easier to annotate reliably using genomic evidence (for example, known variations in the genome leading to cancer). Long reads are also more uniquely tied to their genomic location, so a model can learn that location from the read itself. In contrast, short-read models collapse without explicitly provided context about position in the genome.

4. Scaling to long reads is a prerequisite for a reason beyond accuracy—it seems to be the only way to support inference across the entire genome. Focusing on a limited set of informative regions is short-sighted, because it limits achievable total coverage and disregards new potential evidence from reads beyond the original selection. In sequencing, coverage is measured in depth and breadth. Coverage depth means the number of reads that cover a target nucleotide, usually averaged across regions. Coverage breadth, by contrast, counts the number of base pairs with target coverage depth. High enough coverage is required for statistical power. The sequencing process is stochastic in its choice of genomic regions and can only be directed to some extent, so reaching high coverage depth on a small set of selected regions takes much time, costs more, and limits potential applications. For example, intraoperative diagnostics requires turnaround time in minutes, which would not be feasible under such conditions. A good method is required to produce tangible outputs for any sequence, regardless of its position in the genome, and to differentiate between reads with high and low informational content. Factoring in all reads without severe filtering increases effective coverage breadth and accumulates evidence faster.

5. Finally, a good method must work with a single input source when necessary and with combined multi-omics signals when available. The assumption is that crosstalk between assays is context-specific: several signals may be redundant under one set of conditions and complementary or even orthogonal in another. If we train separate models for each modality, we risk missing these important interactions and recovering incomplete or erroneous associations between specific patterns and cell type or state.
In summary, a good tumor ecosystem deconvolution method is scalable to hundreds of cell types, pattern-specific, works on short- and long-read libraries, can be applied to entire genomes, and can use whatever cell-informative signal is available. No, “good” is not the right word; it would be perfect. No single method checks all the boxes, and no wonder—it is a tall order. Plenty of methods scale to a dozen cell types and could work with hundreds, assuming reliable reference. Still, they're all unimodal, use averaged signal instead of pattern-specific signal, and work within the curated set of regions or markers. Pattern-specific methods are scarce and, until recently, only three had been published; all relied on marker selection and applied only to short reads, primarily in the binary context (tumor purity estimation), though one demonstrated deconvolution up to five cell types.a Two use more than one modality (DNA sequence plus methylation). A recent preprint7 introduced another pattern-specific method that applies to long reads and, as a result, is not constrained by a predefined set of regions, yet collapses with short reads, uses only methylation, and is also binary. 
In our paper, we mainly focus on a common limitation of previous pattern-specific methods: scalability across many cell types. We propose a solution that scales three previous methods from a binary to a multi-label setting. We also propose an implementation of the framework that can support further development into a method satisfying all desirable criteria stated above.
Part IIThe problem

One green strip

Imagine you are a tiny animal with sensory input limited to a strip of 8 × 1 pixels. Let’s say that you feed on green apples, breed in the green grass, and are afraid of green snakes for some instinctive reason. Everything else is inconsequential, so from your tiny animal perspective, telling apples, grass, and snakes apart is key to a happy life. One of these days you are out there, minding your business, when suddenly the next picture fills your view.
What should you do? Freeze in fear, run ahead and fight for your love, or reach for cutlery (apparently, you have manners, too). Luckily, this is not your first day living, and you have seen this green strip before — on numerous occasions it was a perfectly juicy apple, and a couple of times you have had a good time in the grass. Was it ever a sign of a snake? You don't recall; probably not; definitely not a snake; must be an apple, then! Where is my fork? You reach for your cutlery and take a step forward, only to be eaten by a large green snake the next instant.  
Reborn the next evening (yes, reincarnation is a thing where you live, and you conveniently retain all your memories), you ponder what happened. “Perhaps I was making my decisions too hastily, and I should tread more lightly the next time.” You realize your biggest mistake was basing your decision on a single, limited observation, so next time you see a green strip, you decide to watch it longer and look around for other cues. And unless you are very hungry, you stay hidden until you are absolutely sure what you see is one of three things: a snake, an apple, or a blade of grass. Then you label each new partial observation and update your view of the world accordingly. Of course, sooner or later you'll need to act before you can be certain; otherwise, someone else may beat you to dinner, but at least you will try to get a few observations and score them first before assessing your chances.     
So, you adopt this new system, and after three complete observations, you decide it is time to put it to practice. You look seven times; four out of seven are most likely an apple, and the remaining three point to a snake. Risky, but you are so hungry! You must advance! “Thanks-s-s-ssss,” you could have heard if you had ears. Alas, you are wrong this time, again. It was a snake all right. 

LEARNING "HARD" FROM CONTRADICTIONS IS HARD INDEED

The strategy the tiny animal adopted in our whimsical example is not computationally equivalent, but close in spirit, to what would happen if each partial observation were labeled by source and a neural classifier were trained using stochastic gradient descent and mini-batches.
Think about how the algorithm acquires knowledge. It adjusts its associations according to the evidence it sees in each batch. For example, if in the first mini-batch every green strip is assigned to a snake, then the algorithm is satisfied: there is no doubt that the strip belongs to a snake. But in the second mini-batch there are examples in which the same green strip is shown half in snakes and half in apples, leading to confusion: the loss goes up and the gradient changes direction.
Apply this reasoning throughout the entire training process. Features which occur repeatedly and almost solely in one category are more easily assigned to the right class, so the algorithm is more likely to establish the associated link. On the other hand, features that are equally likely to occur in several categories give conflicting evidence. The gradients for these features can cause the model to move in different directions from one mini-batch to the next and may therefore lead to unstable learning or inadequate convergence when using suboptimal training parameters. The imbalance among the classes can also bias the representation learned from the data.
The more different sources/classes a feature can correspond to, the more difficult it becomes for the learner to link it with the right source, and therefore its final solution—if it ever converges—may remain a long way from the true answer. This is the thing with phenomena where true mapping between features and targets is not many-to-one but many-to-many. In the many-to-one case, crisp labels are expected: if you give a computer vision learner a picture of a creature that is undoubtedly a kitten, then classifying it accordingly is not wrong. As for the tiny creature problem, confidently labeling each piece of partial evidence is harmful. Instead, we can provide an algorithm with a "soft" target, effectively shifting from classification problem to a kind of label distribution learning. The panel below gives a simplified example. 

FROM GREEN STRIPS TO GENOMIC READS

As in our green strip example, genomic reads are partial elements of a single observation formed by applying a sequencing technique to a target biological sample. The collection of reads from one experiment is called a read library. In such libraries, each read can be attributed to a single cell type if we apply bulk sequencing to a purified sample with a known target, or if we use single-cell or single-nucleus sequencing, where each read can be traced to a specific cell. In our chosen task, cell-type deconvolution, unlike the green strip example, we do not try to predict the probability that a whole library of reads belongs to a single category. Yet, a deconvolution approach that relies on information from individual reads similarly requires a computational component that scores them — a read classifier.  The probability of a cell type given a read is a common choice of scale, and if one decides to train such a classifier, they need to join many such libraries to facilitate learning. As the number of possible cell types grows, complications from unstable learning and class imbalance accumulate.
Previously, when discussing a toy example with a green strip, we exaggerated the potential detrimental effects of hard labels: with three possible classes, an algorithm is more likely to converge to a softer solution than the one shown, because its loss surface is relatively simple.  
Yet in our case, we have two serious difficulties:
  1. We need to scale from binary to dozens, then to hundreds of classes.
  2. In any given region, all but a few patterns (typically one) are ambiguous across cell types. As we add classes, the share of informative reads, and with it the average information per read, keeps falling.    
To understand why it happens, we need to examine the nuances of DNA methylation distribution across the human genome and how one selects data for training. If we sequence the entire human genome and look at the resulting CpG methylation, represented by roughly thirty million CpGs, the absolute majority of them will show the same methylation level regardless of the cell type. For example, transposable elements, highly methylated domains, and CpG islands that cover up to 80% of all CpGs are non-discriminatory (either methylated or unmethylated in all cell types), and even segments with variable methylation may fluctuate for reasons unrelated to cell type. In practice, this means that if we pick a relatively short DNA fragment at random and try to assign it to a distinct cell type based on its methylome, we will fail most of the time. This is especially true for short reads (see Part I): the longer the read, the higher the chance it will cross a specific region, and vice versa. Cell-type-specific regions are a kind of differentially methylated region (DMR). DMRs are computed from annotated data and can be used to differentiate one cell type vs. all, one cell-type group vs. another cell-type group, and one cell type vs. another cell type. Most of the cell-type-specific DMRs are hypomethylated - that is, the overall methylation level of CpGs in the target cell types is close to zero. Finally, some regions remain discriminatory for cell type and cell state, especially in disease. Still, these are not as widely used, were not our focus in this paper, and we mention them here only because we believe nuanced region analysis will be essential for our future work.        
To learn strong associations between cell type and the methylome, a classifier must be trained on a sufficient number of informative reads. For short-read libraries, it is common to focus only on DMRs and filter out everything else. This is equivalent to considering each read that does not overlap with a DMR as uninformative or equally probable across target modeled cell types. What remains has a better signal-to-noise ratio. The experiments we feature in our paper use this common approach as well. When applied to a binary setting, as in previously published pattern-specific methods, this results in a clear dichotomy — every read that is not representative of one cell type or group is automatically representative of another. With three cell types, it already breaks. Computationally, you might have a situation where reads from a first cell type are fully unmethylated, reads from a second cell type are fully methylated, and reads from a third cell type have an average methylation level of exactly fifty percent. In practice, you are much more likely to land on a situation where, in a given region, the reads from one cell type are hypomethylated while reads from the other two cell types are hypermethylated and indistinguishable. In the binary case, knowing the methylation pattern fully resolves cell types in an ideal scenario; with three cell types, it only does so 33% of the time (assuming full class balance). Extrapolate further, and you’ll appreciate the increased complexity of scaling to many cell types.
If you keep original labels for each read and try to apply "hard logic" as in our green strip example above, the learning objective will be so ambiguous that your classifier will take a “shortcut” and quickly converge to predicting a uniform vector regardless of the pattern. In their work, Jeong et al.11 used a trick to reshape the learning objective from directly predicting the class of a read to first predicting whether a read is cell-type specific for its region and then resolving the cell type from region targets (because each region in their reference was strictly specific to a single cell type). The corresponding paper features an example with five cell types. We originally overlooked it, and when we later applied it to our setting with 39 cell types, the model converged. However, the final deconvolution results using the classifier predictions were still considerably worse than one of the baselines we had compared at the time. Also, this approach wouldn’t work for regions that aren't one-vs-all, and we wanted a method that would be region-agnostic once we switch to long reads, for which providing explicit DMR context might not be necessary. As such, before our work, scaling pattern-specific, read-level classification-based cell type deconvolution to many cell types remained an unsolved problem.
A NOTE ON THE 39 CELL TYPES

From here on, we will frequently refer to the 39 cell types from the methylation atlas of healthy human cell types by Loyfer et al.10, as it is our reference of choice for testing our methods in the published paper. The dataset includes 205 biological samples from which we recovered reads for modeling, as well as a set of markers—hypomethylated DMRs specific to each covered cell type. This does not mean what we propose is limited to this single atlas, as other references may be used in the future; at the same time, 39 is a sufficient departure from the prevalent binary case in the number of supported cell types and a good target for evaluation. We return to the concrete experiments in Part IV.

Part IIITHE SOLUTION

ONE WAY TO SCALE HARD LABELS

Before presenting the main contribution of our paper, we should mention another solution we came up with, which extends the method of Jeong et al. mentioned earlier. The method is simple: when we take a read, we keep its label corresponding to the biological sample only if it falls within the genomic region specific to the cell type the sample covers. We assign the remaining reads a new, separate label.b This way, we avoid the impossible task of differentiating within uninformative reads while keeping the original labels for informative reads, allowing the model to output target class probabilities directly. In retrospect, we found it conceptually similar to foreground-background separation in object detection, where the learning task is steered toward differentiating among a finite set of target objects or object types, with everything else falling into the background. Hence, we call the added label “a background class” and the method's output: hard labels with background. 

Indeed, when we trained two candidate classifiers on the original labels, both oscillated around high initialization losses and never converged. Adding a background class reliably fixed the problem for one classifier, Dismir. For MethylBERT, we needed to pair it with either changes to the target loss function (applying focal loss)12 or a minibatch-balanced sampling technique to guarantee convergence. The latter proved more efficient, so we adopted it for the entire study.    
So, in hard labels with background, we found a working solution; why not stop there? Our intuition was that clumping all reads into one background class is a crude compression step, because it buries useful variance within a single, undifferentiated group. The thing is that the regions vary in their degree of specificity — even with the simplest region selection scheme that favors black-and-white separation between a target cell type and everything else, there are more and less separable cases. One way to measure this is through what we call a proxy gap specificity scorec. Suppose we work with a reference set of regions, each hypomethylated in a target cell type. At the same time, we have a set of purified biological samples, each represented by a library of genomic reads, with every read in a given sample originating from one known cell type. Then, for each cell-type-specific region, we calculate the fraction of hypomethylated reads in samples that match this region's cell-type target (on-target fraction). Then, we calculate the fraction of hypomethylated reads in samples that do not match this region’s cell-type target; we do this separately for each remaining cell-type group of samples and take the maximum fraction across the resulting values (maximal off-target fraction). Finally, we subtract the maximal off-target fraction from the on-target fraction—this is the proxy gap specificity score. A perfect separation yields a score of exactly 1, meaning an unmethylated read in a given region is observed in only one target cell type in the data. However, this is not the case in the real reference. Take a single region that is supposedly characteristic of adipocytes; not only will you find that the fraction of unmethylated reads found in adipocyte samples is 73%, but also that endothelial cells, erythrocyte progenitors, and oligodendrocytes are unmethylated in the same genomic regions in 23–27% of cases. You take another adipocyte region; this time, the fraction of unmethylated reads in adipocyte samples is 100%, great! Alas, the second-largest fraction comes from heart fibroblasts, at 60%.
One may object that we measure specificity by considering a limited set of patterns reflected in the score. When we look only at unmethylated reads based on criteria like an average read methylation threshold, we may not recover the full separation potential. But even at the pattern level, perfect separation is not achievable, and as we move to more granular reference levels, patterns tend to overlap more and more. In contrast to object detection, where background is noise we want to get rid of, here background contains additional mutual information. This is exactly the many-to-many case, and it raises the question: how do we encode every cell type a single pattern may point to? 

Data-Driven Soft Labels

The idea of reshaping a learning objective by changing the target from a single label to a representative vector is not novel and falls under its own machine learning task—label distribution learning. Xin Geng coined the term in 2016 in his seminal work with the same title14, along with three main principles differentiating it from classification: 1) the label distribution is a natural part of the original data; 2) the overall label distribution is a goal of learning; 3) the performance should be measured by similarity or distance between the learned distribution and the target.
The first principle can be fulfilled in many ways, which fall into two categories: either explicitly provided with data, typically by aggregating human annotations, like the distribution of discrete movie scores on IMDb; or computed in a domain-specific or more general way. One example of domain-specific computation would be deriving gene expression for genes associated with various diseases, where, for each gene, its expression vector is computed for each target disease and transformed into a target label distribution via normalization. An example of a general algorithm is Graph Laplacian Label Enhancement by Ning Xu15, which recovers the label distribution by leveraging hard-labeled data and exploiting associations between different labels by analyzing the feature space. Our proposed method falls into the group of general methods, as it applies to any many-to-many mapping between feature vectors and label distributions where each feature vector is already paired with a single label.   
GENERAL FORMULATION OF DATA DRIVEN SOFT LABELS

Let x be an observed feature (a pattern, a patch, any partial piece of evidence) and let c range over the labels it can belong to. Let nc(x) be the number of times x appears with label c in the reference data, and let wc be a weight for label c. The soft label for x is the weighted, normalized count vector

qc(x) = wc nc(x) / Σk wk nk(x)

With wc = 1, q(x) is the maximum-likelihood estimate of P(c | x) in the reference as collected. With wc ∝ 1/Nc, where Nc is the total amount of data for label c, q(x) is the maximum-likelihood estimate of P(c | x) in a balanced reference, one in which every label is equally represented. This corrects for labels that are over- or under-sampled.

If a feature is particular to a single label, then q(x) takes the form of a one-hot vector and the soft label becomes a normal hard label; but if the feature is shared then q(x) is distributed among all the labels which produce it in proportion to how frequently each one does.

In our specific problem, x is a read’s methylation pattern spanning a specific genomic locus, c is a cell type, and Nc is the cell type’s total coverage.

The method is simple and intuitive. Indeed, when working with sequencing libraries where each read can be directly attributed to its cell of origin, one may compute data-driven soft labels trivially. 

Vanilla data-driven soft labels are not without limitations. One immediate problem is patterns with low overall coverage—if a pattern appears only a few times in the data, its counts lack statistical power, and the derived soft label will be noisy and not representative of the true label distribution. Low coverage can be a property of the data, but it can be exacerbated further by changes in how we define the pattern. For our experiments, we represent each read at labeling as a sequence of binary CpG calls, but one may switch to continuous methylation confidence scores when working with ONT or PacBio data, which makes every read unique under the vanilla scheme. The probability of obtaining unique reads also increases when increasing read length.  
To account for low coverage in existing data and potential causes of low coverage in the future, we extended data-driven soft labels with a pooling mechanism. The idea of pooling is simple — for each case where the count of a particular observed feature pattern is low, the coverage may be increased by adding counts from similar feature patterns. In our paper, we propose a specific implementation of this pooling idea, using Jaccard distance to measure similarity between the target and pooling-candidate methylation patterns. For our method, one defines a minimum count threshold below which pooling applies and a maximum Jaccard distance beyond which a pair is no longer considered similar. The method adds counts from the closest pooling candidate to the furthest until it either reaches the count threshold or runs out of candidates.      
By construction, data-driven soft labels capture the available information in every read, but it can't make truly uninformative reads more surprising. Reads that fall into the background will have near-uniform soft labels, with entropy close to the maximum. And with many classes, it reintroduces the class imbalance problem, this time with high-entropy reads dominating mini-batch data observed at training time. Left unattended, the model will struggle to converge without taking a shortcut and predicting uniform labels to satisfy most of the data. One approach that came to mind was to solve for a feasible learning-hyperparameter range, but we dismissed it as too constrained and inefficient. Instead, we decided to weight our evidence by how confidently we can attribute an instance to a single label. The rationale was that instances strongly associated with a single class are a target signal that should be preserved. In contrast, instances with near-uniform label distribution are non-informative and represent true background that can be disregarded. Anything in between is down-weighted in proportion to how far its highest label probability falls below 1. Based on this logic, we introduced a confidence-weighted cross-entropy loss, which we used for training. In retrospect, we found it to be conceptually inverse to the one designed by Jing Wang and Xin Geng16, where they upweight examples with higher entropy.

SYTO - A MODULAR READ-LEVEL DECONVOLUTION FRAMEWORK

Soft labels let the classifier learn more nuanced label distributions for individual reads, preserving more per-read information. The deconvolution step is applied to the classifier output. Previous methods implemented the deconvolution step using variants of Maximum Likelihood Estimation. We do not find this approach generally correct because classifier confidence scores are not guaranteed to be well-calibrated measures of true probabilities, especially for neural networks. We also believe the family of possible deconvolvers could be extended with unsupervised and supervised methods alike, since the true mapping between classifier outputs and cell-type proportions may be nonlinear. Finally, one can further calibrate the deconvolution results on unseen data to correct learned biases. We combined these three stages—classification, deconvolution, and calibration — into a unified modular framework, Syto, named for the Ukrainian word for a sieve. 

The scheme below shows how reads pass through Syto end to end, at training and at inference; the following subsections discuss each element in turn. 

Extended read-level classifiers

The first stage of Syto is to apply a read-level classifier to assign cell-type probabilities to each read. In our paper, we included three existing classifiers, supplemented with extensions to support multi-label fitting, plus a new, deliberately simple classifier that memorizes each unique methylation pattern and its label from the data and predicts new reads by resolving the label from the resulting lookup table. Among previously published classifiers included in Syto source code are our implementation of CancerDetector17, as well as our extensions of Dismir18 and MethylBERT11. Syto is not limited to these options; you can plug in any other classifier that produces a score vector that sums to one and matches the required shape. It does not enforce labeling schemes, and the classifier can be non-pattern-specific; for example, CancerDetector models cell-type probabilities given reads using beta-binomial distributions over average read methylation levels.

PASSING CLASSIFIER SCORES TO DECONVOLVER

As you might recall, each read in the library is partial evidence and a very weak predictor of the constituent cell types. Only when you account for information from many reads can you apply a deconvolution step. One thing to decide is how to pass the predictions to the deconvolver. One way is to propagate scores as-is, but that means each read must be custom-weighted, which quickly becomes computationally prohibitive. A sensible approach is to aggregate information from multiple reads, for example, by taking a simple average. This is similar in spirit to average pooling common in convolutional neural networks. In our case, a weighted average scheme is more appropriate, where the weight is proportional to the number of CpGs each read covers — this way, the read that overlaps eight CpGs contributes twice as much as the read that overlaps only four. In principle, one can aggregate the predictions from the entire library, resulting in a single vector covering all possible classes. This, however, will flatten the signal because most reads are uninformative. Grouping by cell-type-specific regions, or by regions with similar feature-target associations, yields a much better signal-to-noise ratio within each group. The resulting G × C matrix is a first step in compressing classifier output before deconvolution.       
When looking at the resulting G × C matrices, we observed differences between labeling schemes. Hard labeling with background produces matrices in which only the diagonal and background-class columns carry strong signal, with off-diagonal entries very close to zero. This is expected by design, as information about the label distribution of cell-type subsets that are not specific to the region from which the read is drawn is clumped into the background class. The data-driven soft labeling scheme produces a different pattern—the diagonal signal remains very strong but is attenuated in proportion to cell-type specificity based on the reference regions. Furthermore, the off-diagonal elements of the matrix also emit signal, encoding real crosstalk between cell types in the data. 
As our analysis shows, the information distribution within aggregated feature matrices depends on the choice of labeling scheme. Even with soft labels, the features can be further filtered without losing discriminatory power. In references where most cell types are well separated within their regions, crosstalk is minimal, so only a few off-diagonal elements remain informative after aggregation. In our experiment, using only the 156 most relevant of the 39 × 39 = 1,521 matrix values achieves 99.95% of the performance compared to using the full feature matrix.  

Pseudobulk generation

A deconvolver learns a mapping between an aggregated prediction matrix and the corresponding cell-type proportions, so it needs many matrices with known true proportions. Generating enough real mixtures with known ground truth is an undertaking no small lab can afford, and public sources offer no alternatives either. Pseudobulks—synthetically generated mixtures based on known purified references—are a common solution to this problem. Syto features pseudobulk generation logic that generates corresponding aggregated feature matrices from the classified libraries.  

Deconvolution

As described earlier, the deconvolution step is just a mapping between aggregated classifier predictions and the corresponding cell type proportions. This means that one may largely adapt many existing methods that work with beta or alpha values. For example, in our paper, we include the Non-Negative Least Squares (NNLS) constrained linear regression, a backbone of many published deconvolution algorithms. We also feature another unsupervised variant, Probability Simplex Least Squares (PSLS), which enforces outputs to sum to one. We could not include them all in the paper, but adding them is straightforward.

To assess the feasibility of learning a transfer function, we also feature three supervised methods: Extreme Gradient Boosting Decision Trees (XGB) and two small neural classifiers with slightly different architectures (SWN and MLP).     

CALIBRATION

We originally considered the calibration problem in relation to classification outputs, motivated by evidence in the paper “On Calibration of Modern Neural Networks” by Chuan Guo et al.19 and subsequent research. Our idea was to align predicted and ground-truth label distributions more closely, such that the obtained scores are better representative of the true probabilities. This would have let us use maximum likelihood estimation as the deconvolution step. Later, we shifted to applying calibration as a post-deconvolution step, based on the observation that our methods and baselines we have compared to all share a linearly correctable bias in their deconvolution results. We believe this bias arises from the reference all methods share; therefore, calibration in Syto is designed to correct it. In our paper, we implemented three hyperparameter-free calibration schemes that can be applied in standalone mode. We did not have time to return to the original idea: calibrating classifier outputs and measuring its influence on the end-to-end results remains an open problem.   
Part IVTHE FINDINGS

EXPERIMENTAL RESULTS

We ran two experiments. Both used the Loyfer et al. atlas for training, as briefly mentioned earlier in this blog post. To date, it remains the largest publicly available methylation atlas by the number of unique donors (137) and cell types (39). Since our goal at the time was to scale to the largest viable set of possible labels, this was our choice. The term “atlas” may sound foreign to someone outside the field. Put simply, an atlas here is an annotated dataset that includes raw or aggregated data and metadata, plus processed data sufficient to characterize distinctive methylome patterns in the featured cell types. In this atlas, we had access to summary read statistics that could be used to recover original methylomes exactly, along with a set of informative markers—DMRs—shipped with the publication. The terminology can be confusing: these marker sets are also often referred to as the atlas (in the paper, we use atlas in both senses, depending on context).
A word on our baselines, the existing deconvolution methods we set out to outperform. Syto effectively subsumed all previous pattern-specific methods designed to work on short reads: extended versions of CancerDetector, Dismir, and MethylBERT became choices in our framework. So there was no point comparing, for example, with previous versions of CancerDetector and Dismir that were not designed to run in the non-binary case (as for the original MethylBERT, as we mentioned before, its labeling scheme and deconvolver lagged behind the baselines we eventually chose, so we never included it in the main paper text, but there is an extensive comparison in supplementary B.7, if you want to check it out). Therefore, we selected baselines from non-pattern-specific methods. We compared Syto against four established reference-based deconvolution methods: UXM10, CelFiE21, Houseman CP22, and EpiDISH23, all of which have demonstrated strong performance in previous benchmarks24,25,26.
The first experiment examined in-sample performance by splitting the atlas into training and held-out test samples from non-overlapping donors. We carried these sets end-to-end, from classification to calibration, to avoid information leakage. Following the framework, we trained various classifiers, then generated pseudobulks by mixing with random target proportions, different for each set. We fitted deconvolvers on those pseudobulks and calibrators on the deconvolvers' output. We tested 360 Syto combinations in total, plus 4 external baselines. Additionally, we applied various calibration schemes to baselines to see whether calibration can be used outside Syto to improve existing model results.   
In this first experiment, the best Syto configuration reduces mean squared error on the predicted proportions from 3.28 to 0.88 (×10⁻⁴) against the strongest baseline — a 3.7-fold reduction — and reaches an R² of 98.9%.

Pseudobulks are a common way to test models, but they are a bit like giving students practice questions from the textbook written by the tutor who also wrote the exam. Even separating different donors between splits cannot guarantee generalization beyond this dataset. The ideal test would be to deconvolve samples with known, reliable ground truth; alas, we could neither generate nor find such data at the time.
We ended up using a different methylation atlas as held-out test set, made with another sequencing method, that included 16 types of human tissue from 260 samples27. We didn't have ground truth for these tissues, but we could use another external reference built with single-cell data28 to define a set of constituent cell types for each target tissue, still without actual target proportions. That gave us an opportunity to compute a surrogate performance measure—the sum of predicted cell-type proportions from the target set. In a perfect algorithm, it would have been 100%, and in a perfectly wrong one, 0%. We called it the Tissue Concordance Score (TCS). The caveat is that TCS is blind to errors within the correct set of cell types, so a score of 80% means it is at least 20% wrong but could be much more wrong. So, it is an imperfect, upper-bound measure of an achievable performance. For this reason, we also analyzed TCS failure modes extensively (supplementary E.7). Overall, we concluded that TCS on out-of-distribution data is an informative complement to pseudobulk-based performance measures, which showed no major issues under our specific experimental conditions, but it must be rigorously profiled whenever used in reporting.  

In the second experiment, Syto also improved on the baselines: its best configuration scored 12.8 points above the best one.

WHAT WE SAW THROUGH PLATONIAd

The 360 configurations create a limited set of fixed alternatives; by considering them together across multiple metrics, you can see interesting patterns emerging. These are the insights that caught our attention.

1. The labels matter more than the classifier choice. The three pattern-specific classifiers in our research—previously published Dismir and MethylBERT, and our simple Lookup classifier—are grounded in different inductive biases and vary substantially in capacity and complexity. All of them have demonstrated different learning abilities, reflected in their performance, but the gap between results was explained more by the choice of labeling scheme than by the choice of classifier. With hard labels, Lookup, as expected, lagged behind the much more sophisticated Dismir and MethylBERT in both experiments. Once we switched to soft labels, it achieved comparable performance. MethylBERT, when trained on hard labels, dropped significantly on the TCS scale despite performing relatively well on pseudobulks. We observed no such drop for data-driven soft labels. We believe computing labels this way substantially simplifies the classifier's job.

2. Calibration might be the way to improve existing deconvolvers. The baselines we examined all showed a linearly correctable bias, for example, consistently underpredicting the proportion of colon fibroblasts, a cell type with the least data in the reference (only three marker regions versus 25 for each other cell type) and the least specific markers. It turns out that calibrating them on pseudobulk mixtures constructed from a subset of the reference data achieves better alignment between predicted and ground-truth proportions, which carries over to out-of-distribution data, as we observe in our second experiment. As such, it can be a computationally cheap way to improve the performance of existing deconvolvers and, in the long run, adapt to specific tasks without changing the reference. One could imagine, for example, calibrating for a different sequencing source with its own technical biases or for a target tissue. We also saw that the existing vector calibrator and our two proposed linear calibrators, with clip and normalize and with simplex projection, have characteristics that are more or less desirable depending on the application. For example, the simplex method enforces sparsity in the results, while the vector method allocates more mass to the dominating component.  

3. Most of the gains from pattern-level information are yet to be captured. Our extension of CancerDetector, which uses average methylation per read and discards the within-read pattern entirely, ranked second among uncalibrated configurations. If you factor in the environment in which CancerDetector was tested, it is not surprising that it performed well—the utilized marker set is optimized to separate reads based on the number of close-to-fully and fully unmethylated reads, with methylated reads on the opposite end. Our extended CancerDetector models average methylation values per read for each genomic region and class as a Beta Distribution; therefore, its probabilistic model matches the marker set well, where the alpha value alone is informative by design. Accepting this result as evidence of parity between alpha-value-based methods and pattern-specific methods would be a grave mistake. We believe pattern-specificity gains are yet to be seen and will emerge when applied to more nuanced atlases needed to achieve higher granularity in cell typing and longer reads, where alpha-value alone won’t be sufficient to discriminate between classes.  

LIMITATIONS AND THE NEXT STEPS

If you followed this blog post through, you may already see that we are far from the perfect deconvolution method we aim for. Data-driven soft labels pave the way to scaling to many cell types, but we still need more prerequisite work to accommodate hundreds of normal cell types and a wide range of tumor types and subclones. The task demands unprecedented data generation and new methods to identify pattern-specific markers, as well as methods to integrate bulk tumor samples into a common tumor-normal reference. Scaling to long reads, by contrast, is a solved problem; efficient architectures exist that process much longer sequences than those in our paper. Yet a single read-length-agnostic configuration capable of simultaneously achieving strong results on long and short reads interchangeably remains to be created. Needless to say, multimodality, which is one of the hottest topics in computational biology, is another aspect still lacking. Bringing together methylation, chromatin accessibility, and histone marks may expand differentiation limits, allowing discrimination of cell types that are inseparable when analyzed from a single angle alone. So the problem we claimed to resolve is already focused and limited in scope. Within that scope there are further limitations, things we could have done or covered that relate directly to scaling to many cell types. We did not have ground-truth out-of-distribution data for validation, so we came up with TCS, which is far from perfect and invites concerns that only rigorous argument and detailed analysis can address; we did both in the paper. Validation on real mixtures is still needed and will be done in subsequent research. Another aspect of our research relates to the choice of atlases, which, while having its merits, is not ideal in terms of resolution and source—many samples in this atlas are derived from tumor-adjacent normal tissues, which raises concerns about applicability for normal cell-type deconvolution. The reference samples also have purity issues; they are not strictly 100% pure, and 17 out of 205 are below 80%. This means the reference is contaminated and the derived labels are noisy, which makes pseudobulk targets noisy too.  

Our ultimate goal is to enable tumor ecosystem deconvolution. In this paper, we covered only normal cell types. We foresee many applications of Syto, from cancer detection to longitudinal monitoring of disease and treatment progression in liquid and solid biopsies. In this publication, we wanted to demonstrate that scaling to multiple cell types for pattern-specific methods is unlocked. The path to clinical application now hinges on our ability to integrate malignant data from target tumors.
